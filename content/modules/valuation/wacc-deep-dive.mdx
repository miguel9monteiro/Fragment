A weighted average cost of capital is not one number. It is a sequence of
six choices, each defensible on its own. If a senior member challenges your
WACC, they are not challenging the result — they are testing whether you
made the choices on purpose.

This module walks each of the six in turn, gives you the defensible answer,
and shows you the question that will follow if you give the lazy one.

## The formula and the six choices

<FormulaBlock
  formula="WACC = \frac{E}{V} \cdot r_e + \frac{D}{V} \cdot r_d \cdot (1 - t)"
  caption="Each variable on the right is a choice. Which one is the easiest to attack?"
/>

The six:

1. The **risk-free rate** that anchors the cost of equity.
2. The **equity risk premium** — the expected return investors demand above
   risk-free for taking equity risk.
3. The **beta** that scales the equity risk premium to this company.
4. The **cost of debt** — marginal, not historical.
5. The **weights** — market-based, current or target.
6. The **tax rate** that turns interest into an after-tax cost.

A clean WACC defends all six. A weak WACC defends one and fudges five.

## Choice 1 — risk-free rate

Use the on-the-run 10-year sovereign yield in the company's reporting
currency, as of the valuation date. For US issuers, the 10-year Treasury.

<DeepDive title="Why 10-year, not 30-year or 5-year?">
The conventional answer is duration matching: cash flows in a DCF stretch
out roughly a decade, so use a 10-year. The fuller answer is that the
10-year is the most liquid point on the curve, the least distorted by
short-term Fed policy, and the standard reviewers will expect to see. If you
deviate, lead with the deviation: "we used the 30-year because the cash
flows are unusually long-dated, here's why."
</DeepDive>

## Choice 2 — equity risk premium

Use a published implied equity risk premium — Damodaran's monthly update is
the club default. Avoid historical realised premiums (they whipsaw with the
window), and avoid academic textbook numbers (they're stale).

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't average everything">
A common mistake is to "split the difference" between two ERPs by averaging
them. Pick one approach and stick with it. Reviewers will ask "why this
number?" — "because it's the average of two" is not a thesis.
</Callout>

## Choice 3 — beta

Three steps, in order:

1. **Build a peer set.** Five to eight pure-play peers. Justify each
   inclusion in one sentence.
2. **Pull each peer's levered beta** from a 5-year weekly regression against
   the local broad market index. Bloomberg's `RBETA` or a hand-rolled
   regression against the S&P 500 both work.
3. **Unlever each peer's beta** to remove its capital structure, take the
   median, and re-lever to your target's capital structure.

<FormulaBlock
  formula="\beta_{u} = \frac{\beta_{l}}{1 + (1 - t) \cdot D/E}"
  caption="Hamada equation. Strip out the financial leverage to get the asset beta."
/>

<SelfCheck question="A senior says: 'Why didn't you just use the company's own regression beta?' What's the right answer?">
Single-stock regression betas are noisy — the standard error is often big
enough to swing WACC by 50–100 bps. Pulling the peer-median unlevered beta
and re-levering reduces that noise dramatically, and it forces you to
think about which businesses are actually comparable on a risk basis. If the
company has a long, clean trading history and a stable business, you can
cross-check against its own regression — but the peer median should be
your default.
</SelfCheck>

## Choice 4 — cost of debt

The marginal cost of debt is the rate at which the company would borrow
today. Three sources, in order of preference:

- **Yield to maturity on the company's most recent senior unsecured bond.**
  Best signal.
- **Synthetic rating + credit spread**, when there are no liquid bonds.
  Build an interest-coverage-based rating, look up the median spread for
  that rating, add to the risk-free rate.
- **Last reported borrowing rate**, only if neither of the above is
  available, and only with a caveat in your model.

Never use the historical effective interest rate (interest expense divided by
average debt). It is backward-looking and reflects coupons set years ago at
different rate environments.

## Choice 5 — capital structure weights

Use **current market weights**, not book.

- Equity weight = market capitalisation
- Debt weight = market value of debt (proxied by book value if bonds aren't
  liquid)

If the company is in the middle of a deliberate capital structure shift
(e.g., a leveraged buyback program), it can be defensible to use a "target"
structure — but flag it explicitly and defend the target.

## Choice 6 — tax rate

Use the **marginal statutory rate** in the jurisdiction where incremental
profits would be taxed. Not the effective rate, which is distorted by
one-offs, deferred items, and tax shields.

For US-domiciled companies: 21% federal + ~3–5% blended state, so 23–25%
typically.

## A defensible WACC, in one paragraph

> "Our WACC of 9.2% is built on a 4.2% 10-year Treasury, a 5.5% Damodaran
> implied ERP, and a peer-median re-levered beta of 1.15 from a five-name
> peer group. Cost of debt is the 5.8% YTM on the company's 2032 senior
> unsecured. Weights are market-based, current. We sensitise the fair value
> ±100 bps; the thesis holds at the upper bound."

If you can say that out loud, you can defend the rest of the model.

<Callout type="insight" title="The discipline">
WACC is not a precision tool. It is a credibility instrument. The number
matters less than the process you can show behind it.
</Callout>
