A discounted cash flow model is a translation device. It takes a story about a
business — its growth, its margins, its capital intensity, its risk — and
turns that story into a single number you can defend. The number is only as
good as the story, and the story has to survive a senior member's
cross-examination.

This module walks through the four building blocks of a DCF, the math you
must be able to write on a whiteboard, and the questions every PMC reviewer
will ask before they let you call something a buy.

<Callout type="insight" title="The whole point">
A DCF is not a spreadsheet exercise. It is a structured argument that links
operational assumptions to a present value. If you cannot defend each
assumption out loud, you cannot defend the price.
</Callout>

## The four building blocks

Every DCF you will ever build, on a back-of-envelope or in a 200-row model,
has the same skeleton:

1. **Free cash flow projection** — what cash the business throws off, year by
   year, after the reinvestment it needs to keep growing.
2. **Discount rate** — the cost of capital that reflects how risky those
   cash flows are. We unpack this in the <KeyTerm slug="WACC">WACC</KeyTerm>
   module.
3. **Terminal value** — the present value of everything that happens past
   your explicit forecast horizon, usually via a Gordon growth or exit
   multiple.
4. **Bridge to equity** — net debt, minorities, and other claims that sit
   between enterprise value and what equity holders own.

Get any one of these wrong by 10% and your fair value is wrong by something
between 5% and 50%. The leverage between assumption error and price error is
why every line item matters.

## Free cash flow to the firm

We almost always model unlevered free cash flow (FCFF) — the cash the
business produces before any decision about capital structure:

<FormulaBlock
  formula="FCFF = EBIT \cdot (1 - t) + D\&A - CapEx - \Delta NWC"
  caption="Unlevered free cash flow to the firm. EBIT after tax, plus non-cash charges, less reinvestment."
/>

Two traps that catch first-year analysts:

<Callout type="warning" title="Two common traps">
**Stock-based comp is a real cost.** Adding it back as if it were depreciation
inflates your fair value. Treat it as cash compensation unless you have a
specific reason not to.

**Working capital is not a one-time bump.** A growing business almost always
needs growing working capital. If your ΔNWC line is zero across five forecast
years, your model is wrong.
</Callout>

<SelfCheck question="Why do we use unlevered FCF rather than free cash flow to equity for a typical pitch?">
Because unlevered FCF is independent of capital structure. We discount it at
WACC to get enterprise value, then subtract net debt to get equity value.
This separates the operating thesis from the financing thesis — which is
exactly what we want when arguing the underlying business is mispriced. Levered
FCF discounted at cost of equity is mathematically equivalent in theory, but
mixes the two threads in practice.
</SelfCheck>

## The discount rate

We discount future cash flows at the **weighted average cost of capital** —
the blended return debt and equity holders demand for taking the risk of
financing the business.

<FormulaBlock
  formula="WACC = \frac{E}{V} \cdot r_e + \frac{D}{V} \cdot r_d \cdot (1 - t)"
  caption="WACC. Equity weight times cost of equity, plus debt weight times after-tax cost of debt."
/>

The cost of equity comes from <KeyTerm slug="CAPM">CAPM</KeyTerm>. Cost of
debt is the company's marginal borrowing rate, not its historical interest
expense. Weights are at market values, not book.

For a deep dive on each input — beta, equity risk premium, target capital
structure — see the WACC module.

<SelfCheck question="If a senior member says 'your WACC of 8% feels low', what are the three places you should check first?">
1. **Beta** — are you using a regression that captures the right time period
   and business mix? Re-leveraged from a peer set?
2. **Equity risk premium** — what number are you using and why? 5%? 6%?
   A 100bp move here moves WACC by about 60–80bp.
3. **Capital structure weights** — are you using current market weights or a
   "target" structure? Pick one explicitly and defend it.
</SelfCheck>

## Terminal value

By the end of your explicit forecast (usually year 5 or 10), the business
should be in **steady state**: growing at a rate it can sustain forever,
earning a return on incremental capital roughly equal to its cost of capital.
If those things aren't true at the terminal year, your terminal value is
fragile.

<FormulaBlock
  formula="TV_n = \frac{FCFF_{n+1}}{r - g}"
  caption="Gordon growth terminal value. FCFF in the year after the final forecast year, divided by the spread between WACC and the perpetual growth rate."
/>

Terminal value typically represents 60–80% of total enterprise value in a
DCF. That's a lot of weight on a single assumption. Two sanity checks every
reviewer will run:

- **Implied exit multiple.** Divide your terminal year EBITDA into your
  terminal value. Does the implied EV/EBITDA make sense versus where peers
  trade today?
- **Implied reinvestment rate.** A perpetual growth rate of 3% with a 15% ROIC
  implies you're reinvesting only 20% of NOPAT. Does that match the business?

<KeyTerm slug="DCF">DCF</KeyTerm> models can be made to say almost anything if
you let the terminal value drift. Constraining it forces honesty.

## The bridge to equity

The DCF gives you enterprise value. Members own equity. The bridge:

```
  Enterprise value
- Net debt (debt – cash)
- Minority interest (at fair value, not book)
- Pension underfunding
- Out-of-the-money convertibles, preferreds, etc.
+ Non-operating assets (excess cash, equity investments)
= Equity value
÷ Diluted shares outstanding
= Fair value per share
```

The number you compare to today's price is the last line. Everything before
it is bookkeeping — but bookkeeping that, done sloppily, costs you 5–15% of
fair value.

## What seniors will ask

When you bring a DCF to a pitch room, expect three questions in this order:

1. **What is the implied terminal exit multiple?** If you can't answer in 10
   seconds, you don't know your model.
2. **What growth rate, between today and the terminal year, drives the most
   value?** Show you understand the sensitivity.
3. **At what assumption does the thesis break?** Pick the variable you'd be
   most embarrassed about being wrong on, and have its breakeven number ready.

<Callout type="question" title="Before your next pitch">
Open your model. Can you state — out loud, without scrolling — your terminal
year revenue, EBIT margin, terminal growth rate, WACC, and implied EV/EBITDA?
If not, build a one-page summary tab and rehearse until you can.
</Callout>
