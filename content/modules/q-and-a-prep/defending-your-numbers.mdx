The pitch is the easy part. You wrote it, you rehearsed it, the slides flow
in the order you chose. Q&A is where members are made or unmade. A senior
gets to ask anything — about your model, the company, the industry, your
WACC, the comparable set, the customer mix — in any order, and your answer
has to land in under thirty seconds.

This module is about how to be ready. Not by memorising more facts, but by
knowing where every number in your deck came from, and by practising the
discipline of saying "I don't know" without panicking.

## What seniors actually probe

A senior member is not trying to break your pitch. They are testing whether
you understand your own argument well enough to handle the questions a
client, a portfolio manager, or a sceptic would ask. The probes fall into
four buckets.

<SelfCheck question="What are the four buckets of Q&A questions you should expect?">
1. **Inputs.** "Where does this number come from?" — beta, ERP, market size,
   margin assumption.
2. **Sensitivities.** "What happens if you're wrong about X?" — testing
   whether you understand how the thesis breaks.
3. **Counterfactuals.** "Why isn't the market already pricing this?" —
   testing whether you have a real edge.
4. **Frame.** "Why this stock now, not in six months?" — testing whether you
   know your catalyst.

If you've prepared for all four, almost no question will be a surprise.
</SelfCheck>

## Bucket 1 — input questions

These are the easiest and the most common. The senior wants to confirm you
own every number in your model.

- "Where did the 12% revenue growth come from?"
- "What's your terminal growth rate? Why?"
- "What WACC are you using? Walk me through the build."
- "Where did the peer set come from? Why these names?"

The right preparation: keep a one-page **defence sheet** behind your deck.
Not in the deck — behind it. It lists every assumption with a one-line
rationale and a source. You should never need to flip to it during the
pitch, but you should be able to.

<Callout type="insight" title="The defence sheet test">
Before any pitch, hand your defence sheet to a teammate and ask them to
quiz you on a random row. If you can't answer the rationale in two
sentences, you haven't earned that input.
</Callout>

## Bucket 2 — sensitivity questions

These are slightly harder. The senior is asking how the thesis behaves
under stress.

- "If the gross margin doesn't expand, where does fair value land?"
- "What's your fair value at 8% versus 10% WACC?"
- "How much of the value is in the terminal year?"

The right preparation: build a single sensitivity table on the last page of
your model and **memorise the corners**. You need to know, instantly,
where fair value lands at the bear and bull edges of your two most
load-bearing assumptions.

<SelfCheck question="A senior asks: 'How much of your fair value comes from the terminal value?' Why is this question dangerous?">
Because if the answer is over 80%, your thesis isn't really about the
business — it's about a single perpetual growth assumption. That's a
fragile place to stand. If your TV share is high (which is normal), be
ready to defend it: explain why the explicit forecast captures the
disruptive part of the trajectory and why steady state is plausible. The
question is a test of whether you've thought about that exposure, not a
trap.
</SelfCheck>

## Bucket 3 — counterfactual questions

These separate good analysts from great ones. The senior wants to know why
your view differs from consensus.

- "Why is the market wrong about this?"
- "Sell-side targets are at €40. You're at €55. Where do you think they're
  off?"
- "What does the bear case know that you don't?"

The right preparation: write down, **before you finalise the deck**, the
single most informed bear thesis you can imagine. Not a strawman — the
sharpest version of the opposing view. Then write your two-sentence rebuttal.

If you can articulate the bear case better than your reviewer can, you've
earned the right to disagree with it.

## Bucket 4 — frame questions

The hardest, because they are about judgment.

- "Why this stock and not its closest competitor?"
- "Why now? What changes in the next 12 months?"
- "What's the catalyst that closes the gap?"

The right preparation: have a **catalyst calendar** — a one-line note for
each of the next four quarters with a specific event you are watching. If
your thesis depends on a slow-burn re-rating with no specific catalyst, say
so explicitly. Reviewers respect honesty about the timeline more than they
respect a manufactured catalyst.

## When you don't know

The single most powerful Q&A move is the clean "I don't know."

<Callout type="warning" title="What never to do">
Never bluff. Seniors can hear it. The cost of getting caught making up an
answer is much higher than the cost of admitting a gap. Members who bluff
once lose credibility for the rest of the year.
</Callout>

The right pattern when stumped:

1. **Acknowledge.** "I don't have that number on me."
2. **Bound.** "But I'd expect it to be in the X to Y range, because…"
3. **Commit.** "I'll come back to you with the exact figure today."
4. **Follow through.** Send the answer within 24 hours.

That sequence protects credibility. Bluffing destroys it.

## The senior's hidden test

Most senior questions are not really about the answer. They are about how
you handle the question. Calm, precise, willing to say "I don't know" — that
is the mark of a member who can sit on the buy-side desk in three years.

<SelfCheck question="What's the difference between a confident answer and an arrogant answer?">
A confident answer is grounded in a specific, sourced reasoning chain you
can show. An arrogant answer skips the chain and lands on the conclusion.
When a senior pushes back on a confident answer, you have something to walk
them through. When they push back on an arrogant answer, you have nothing
to fall back on. The distinction matters more than tone.
</SelfCheck>

<SelfCheck question="The senior asks a question you genuinely don't understand. Should you ask them to clarify?">
Yes — every time. "Can I make sure I'm answering the right question? Are
you asking about [interpretation A] or [interpretation B]?" is a sign of
discipline, not weakness. Answering the wrong question wastes everyone's
time and looks worse than asking for clarification.
</SelfCheck>

## A practice ritual

Before any pitch:

1. Write your defence sheet from memory. Cross-check against your model.
2. Run a 15-minute mock Q&A with a teammate who hasn't seen the deck.
3. Capture every question you couldn't answer cleanly. Fix those first.
4. Repeat 24 hours before the pitch.

Members who do this never get blindsided. The questions you couldn't answer
in mock are the questions you'll get in the room.

<Callout type="question" title="Before you walk in">
What is the one question you most hope you don't get asked? That's the
question to prepare for first.
</Callout>
