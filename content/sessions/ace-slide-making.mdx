Most analysts spend their first months at any serious finance role discovering that the work of being right is not the same as the work of being read. You can have the cleanest model and the sharpest insight, and if your slide makes the partner squint, you lose. PMC operates in the same universe. We are an advice business — sometimes with little skin in the game — which means we rely almost entirely on our influencing power. The slide is the instrument.

Thirty minutes that earn back hundreds of hours over the course of a PMC career, and a few habits that quietly separate members who get ahead from those who do not.

## Why care?

Slides are not decoration. For PMC's audience — fellow members, senior reviewers, professors, future managers — the slide is the message. Three reasons make the craft non-negotiable.

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Reason 1" title="Credibility">
    A polished slide reflects polished advice. If our slides look
    amateurish, clients question the quality of our insights — even
    when the underlying work is excellent. Aesthetic choices are
    perceived as analytical choices.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Reason 2" title="Clarity" emphasize>
    Clarity is non-negotiable. Good design cuts through the noise and
    lets our message hit home faster. Every reader has a finite
    attention budget; a clean slide spends it on the argument, not on
    decoding the layout.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Reason 3" title="Engagement">
    Every slide should pull focus. If our visuals do not keep eyes
    open, our message is irrelevant. The choice is not "interesting
    versus accurate" — both are required.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

</PullQuote>

## The eight-step roadmap

The full PMC slide-making process is eight steps, and they are sequential. Skip an early step and the later ones cannot recover the slide. The roadmap divides naturally into two halves: a **story** half (what are you saying, to whom, and why should they care) and an **execution** half (how does the slide carry that story without getting in the way).

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 1" title="Dress up to the occasion">
    Pick the right slide style for the right purpose — Marketing,
    Presenting, or Reading.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 2" title="Start with the story">
    Decide the message of the slide before you open PowerPoint.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 3" title="Extract the so what">
    Find the implication. What is in it for the audience and for the
    main argument.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 4" title="Embrace selling">
    Engineer the slide to hold attention — you are competing with the
    audience's phone.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 5" title="Be minimalist">
    Remove anything that does not earn its space. White space is a
    feature.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 6" title="Be consistent">
    Alignment, fonts, font sizes, colours, and tone of voice — same
    decisions repeated across every slide.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 7" title="Align and revise">
    Balance everything. Symmetry is always preferred when it is
    available.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 8" title="Iterate until perfection" emphasize>
    Great slides are the output of many revisions and second
    opinions, not a single sitting.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

The next four sections walk each pair of steps in detail.

### Step 1 — Dress up to the occasion

Not every slide does the same job, and the right style depends on the job. Three styles sit on a spectrum from **catchiness** at one end to **information** at the other.

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Catchiness end" title="Marketing slide">
    Bold imagery, headline-poster typography, minimal text. The job
    is to get someone to show up — to an event, to a meeting, to read
    further. Think club event posters, recruitment slides, social-
    media announcements.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="The PMC default" title="Presenting slide" emphasize>
    Headline title, sub-headline insight, structured visual content —
    designed for a live presentation where the speaker adds context.
    This is where almost every PMC slide should live. The slide
    conveys the main idea; the presenter adds the value on top.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Information end" title="Reading slide">
    Detailed, self-explanatory content — paragraphs, dense tables,
    full footnotes. The reader is the one extracting meaning, and the
    slide must work without a presenter in the room.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

<Callout type="insight" title="The PMC rule">
For PMC, slides should generally be in the **Presenting style**. They convey the main idea, but the presentation should add value to it. **The exception is the <KeyTerm slug="Appendix">Appendix</KeyTerm>**, which should be self-explanatory — the reader will encounter it without you in the room.
</Callout>

### Steps 2–4 — Curate the story

A great story goes a long way, and three questions force you into one.

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 2" title="Start with the story">
    What is the message I want to pass in this slide? The slide is
    not a container for everything you know about the topic — it is
    one specific argument. Write the argument as a sentence before
    you open PowerPoint.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 3" title="Extract the so what" emphasize>
    What is in it for the audience, and how does it support the main
    message? If the answer is "it's interesting" or "it's something I
    found while researching," it does not earn the slide.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 4" title="Embrace selling">
    Am I hooking the audience or are they on their phones? You are
    competing with everything else fighting for the reader's
    attention. The slide has to win that competition every time.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

### Steps 5–7 — Convey the message

A great story without a good supporting slide is a car without wheels. Three principles keep the slide out of the story's way.

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 5" title="Be minimalist">
    White space is your friend. Remove anything that is not adding
    value — extra borders, decorative shapes, redundant labels,
    secondary axes you don't reference, gridlines that don't help the
    reader. If a reader cannot tell why an element is there, the
    element should not be there.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 6" title="Be consistent">
    Same alignment across slides. Same fonts. Same font sizes for the
    same hierarchy of content. Same colours mapped to the same
    meaning. Same communication style and voice. Inconsistency reads
    as carelessness even when the reader cannot articulate why.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Step 7" title="Align and revise">
    Balance everything. When two pieces of content can be aligned —
    they should be. When the slide can be symmetrical — it should be.
    Symmetry and alignment are not aesthetic preferences; they are
    cognitive aids that let the reader's eye travel the slide
    quickly.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

<PullQuote attribution="On craft">
  A great story without a good supporting slide is a car without wheels.
</PullQuote>

### Step 8 — Do not settle for average

Making great slides is not a one-stop deal. It is a process you must be willing to undertake. Five tips compress decades of practical experience.

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Tip 1" title="Ask for help">
    Get second opinions directly. Great slides are born from great
    feedback. The version in your head is not the version a fresh
    pair of eyes sees.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Tip 2" title="See past slides">
    Do not reinvent the wheel every time. PMC has hundreds of past
    decks; somewhere in there is a slide that solves the layout
    problem you are wrestling with right now.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Tip 3" title="Focus on the titles">
    Short and insightful. The title is the only line many readers
    will absorb. It should state the conclusion of the slide, not
    label the topic of the slide.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Tip 4" title="Use visual clues">
    Pictures, icons, schemes, arrows. The eye finds the visual cue
    before it finds the prose; use that to guide the reader to what
    matters.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Tip 5" title="Use appropriate numbers">
    Be precise — but only until being precise stops being relevant.
    Three decimal places on a market-cap figure is precision theatre.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

## In practice — seven before-and-after case studies

Theory only goes so far. The seven examples below are real PMC slides where the original version had specific, named problems, and the improved version fixed them. The same slide; two different versions; one is in the deck.

### Case 1 — The Kering debt schedule (chart formatting)

The slide was meant to show Kering's projected deleveraging path through 2027.

The **original chart** was an Excel default. Y-axis labelled in raw thousands (0, 1000, 2000, … 9000). No data labels on any bar. All ten bars in the same dark colour, so the eye could not tell which years were actuals and which were forecasts. No headline insight, no annotation, no source.

The **improved version** redesigned the same data in <KeyTerm slug="Think-cell">Think-cell</KeyTerm>:

- Data labels on every bar — 2.0, 7.4, 6.5, 5.0, 7.9, 7.5, 6.8, 5.9, 4.9, 3.8 — in € billions
- Forecast years (2024–2027) in a lighter shade so the actuals/projections split is visible without reading the legend
- A **−49%** annotation arrow connecting 2023 to 2027, making the headline insight self-evident at a glance
- Tighter axis range, no gridlines, cleaner typography

<Callout type="insight" title="The lesson">
The same data can be told well or told badly. Default chart settings will always tell it badly. A few minutes formatting saves the reader a few seconds reading — and a few seconds across a 30-slide deck across a 12-person audience is the difference between holding attention and losing it.
</Callout>

### Case 2 — The Federal Funds Effective Rate (chart formatting)

A long time-series of the Fed Funds rate from 2000 to today.

The **original chart** had monthly date labels on the x-axis (`1/1/00`, `12/1/00`, `11/1/01`, …) — 280 ticks crammed into the space. Generic title "Federal Funds Effective Rate." No annotations identifying historical events. No callouts on the peaks.

The **improved version** told the same story differently:

- Year labels on the x-axis (2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, 2020) instead of monthly ticks
- Annotation labels on the major peaks: **Financial crisis** at 5.02%, **COVID crisis** at the dip, **… crisis?** at the recent 5.33% peak
- Y-axis with percent formatting (0%, 1%, 2% … 7%) instead of raw decimals
- A clear sub-headline: the chart now tells a story instead of just plotting data

<Callout type="warning" title="The trap">
Default Excel charting is built for spreadsheet inspection, not communication. Any chart you paste straight from Excel into a slide without reformatting is a slide you are leaving unfinished.
</Callout>

### Case 3 — Hologic at PMC's Portfolio

A slide that needed to communicate why one of the portfolio's holdings (Hologic, ticker HOLX) was underperforming.

The **original** had four named problems:

1. **No graph readability** — the price line was small, dates rotated, scale unclear
2. **Too texty** — three long bullet paragraphs at the bottom of the slide
3. **Overall flow disturbed** — the eye couldn't tell where to start reading
4. **Title does not represent the slide** — title said "Original First Glance," which describes the document, not the message

The **improved version** rebuilt around the message:

- New title: **"Hologic at PMC's Portfolio"**
- New sub-headline: **"Since joining the portfolio on November 2022, Hologic had a negative return of 4%"** — the conclusion, stated upfront
- A clean stock-price chart from Jan-22 to Oct-23 with three numbered key moments overlaid:
  1. **Purchase of 150 shares at $73.26** — November 18, 2022 (~1% portfolio weight)
  2. **All-time high at $87.88** — May 2, 2023 (+20% upside)
  3. **Holding 150 shares at $70.21** — October 4, 2023 (~1% weight)
- A **−4%** arrow tracing the entry-to-current path
- A "**Why underperforming?**" panel summarising the diagnosis in three bullets:
  - Overreliance on COVID and its aftermath in 2021
  - Unpredictable revenue afterwards
  - Undermanagement by PMC

<Callout type="insight" title="The lesson">
The title is not a label. The title is the conclusion. Every PMC slide should pass the test: *if the reader only reads the title, do they take away the right message?*
</Callout>

### Case 4 — Product portfolio comparison (Gucci vs Louis Vuitton)

A slide comparing the bag collections of Gucci and Louis Vuitton across 2021, 2022, and 2023.

The **original** had named problems even though the concept was right:

- **Conceptually good** (the comparison structure works)
- **Different aspect ratios of pictures** — three Gucci bags at different sizes, three LV bags at different sizes, comparison broken
- **Unbalanced white space** — content drifted across the slide
- **Bullet points not needed** — the visual comparison itself was the argument; the text was redundant
- **Small pictures** — the actual evidence (the bags) was hard to see

The **improved version** kept the structure and fixed the execution:

- Title became: **"Unlike Louis Vuitton, Gucci fails to maintain a coherent style throughout time, hindering its customer retention"** — argument front and centre
- Six images, all the same aspect ratio, sized to fill the comparison grid
- Brand logos in the left column for visual anchors
- Bullet points removed
- A discreet footnote: "Ideally, photos would have no background and purses would be easily comparable. White space could be stretched a bit too thin"

The footnote is itself a teaching moment. The improved slide is honest about its remaining compromises.

### Case 5 — Portfolio fit (Deere)

A slide arguing why Deere should remain in the portfolio.

The **original** had three diagnosed problems:

- **Sources not there** — financial claims with no citation
- **White space unbalanced** — the four catalyst boxes were uneven
- **Rounded bullet points** — visually noisy and inconsistent with PMC standards (square bullets are the convention)

The **improved version** kept the four-catalyst structure but tightened every box:

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Catalyst 1" title="Dividend Payer">
    Paying dividends since 1995, 1.53% Div. Yield, set to keep
    growing in the future.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Catalyst 2" title="High Commodity Prices">
    Historically high prices, set to rebound (−21% YoY) due to
    economic uncertainty.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Catalyst 3" title="Growing Sector">
    Global demand projected to increase at a CAGR of 4.2% between
    2023 and 2033.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Catalyst 4" title="High Liquidity">
    With a current ratio above 2.42, Deere has the resources to deal
    with uncertainty ahead.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

The improved slide also added a footnote source — **Bloomberg, TTM Yield** — closing the citation gap that was the first named problem.

### Case 6 — Financial Analysis (Apple)

A standard PMC financial-analysis slide: full P&L table on the left, margin evolution chart on the right, commentary at the bottom.

The **original** had subtle but real problems:

- The margins chart was a line chart with three lines on top of each other — hard to read
- The chart axis values were redundant with the table
- Commentary was a paragraph rather than structured insight

The **improved version**:

- The margins chart became a clustered bar chart with the three margin lines as bars per year — visually cleaner, easier to compare
- Headers and values rounded consistently (one decimal place), with red parentheses for negative growth and green for positive
- Commentary structured as: **"Steady evolution amid:"** — three bullets — *decline in product sales, currency weakness, increase in services' sales (higher margins)*
- The **2023** column highlighted with a dashed border to mark "this is the column the slide is about"
- New title: **"Despite the sluggish results in FY2023, Apple still managed to beat PMC's expectations"**

The chart improvement is the most teachable moment: when three series have similar magnitudes and similar shapes, **bars are easier to compare than lines**. Lines work when one series clearly dominates or when the time-series is long enough that line continuity matters.

### Case 7 — Sales Breakdown (AAR Corp)

A slide breaking down AAR's revenue by segment and geography.

The **original** had:

- A pie chart with **six segments**, two of which were tiny (Integrated Solutions 3.01%, Expeditionary Services −4.20%, Inter-segment Sales unlabelled)
- A stacked bar chart with three regions, but the values printed inside the bars were the totals, not the segment values
- Bullet point commentary in two long blocks

The **improved version**:

- Pie chart simplified to **three meaningful segments** (Parts Supply 42%, Repair & Engineering 31%, Aviation Services 28%) — the small sub-3% segments grouped or dropped from the visual
- Stacked bar chart with each region's value labelled inside its segment (1.6/0.3/0.1, 1.4/0.2/0.1, 1.4/0.2/0.2, 1.5/0.3/0.2, 1.7/0.4/0.2 in $bn)
- A **+16%** annotation arrow on the 2023→2024 totals
- Bullets compressed to three crisp insights per panel
- Footnote at the bottom explaining the 2023 segment split: *"In 2023, the company decided to split the «Aviation Services» sector into three separate segments: Parts Supply, Repair & Engineering and Integrated solutions for transparency."*
- Source citation: Market Screener AAR Corp

<Callout type="warning" title="The pie-chart trap">
Pie charts with more than three or four segments stop communicating. The eye cannot compare similar-sized wedges accurately, and tiny wedges become noise. If you have six segments, you have either too many segments or the wrong chart.
</Callout>

## Think-cell — the productivity multiplier

Once the principles are clear, the bottleneck becomes execution speed. **<KeyTerm slug="Think-cell">Think-cell</KeyTerm>** is the PowerPoint and Excel add-in that does for slides what a templated DCF does for valuation — collapses the time from idea to finished artefact, while raising the quality floor. It is the standard tool in management consulting and a unique time-saver for slide making and modelling.

The toolkit lives in two main groups inside Think-cell's PowerPoint ribbon.

### Elements — the chart library

Sixteen chart types you can drop into a slide and bind to live data:

- **Stacked Column / Bar** — the default for showing parts-of-a-whole over time
- **100% Column / Bar** — same, but normalised to relative shares
- **Clustered Column / Bar** — for comparing categories side by side
- **Build-Up Waterfall / Build-Down Waterfall** — the consultant favourite for showing additive or subtractive walks
- **Mekko (% Axis)** — width and height both encode information
- **Combination** — bars and lines on the same chart
- **Line / Profile** — time series and discrete profiles
- **Stacked Area / 100% Area** — the area equivalents of stacked bars
- **Mekko (Unit Axis)** — Mekko with absolute units
- **Pie** and **Doughnut** — categorical share
- **Scatter** and **Bubble** — two- and three-dimension correlations
- **Gantt / Timeline** — project schedules and event timelines

Every chart type is tied to a small Excel data table you edit in place — change a number and the chart updates instantly, with consistent formatting.

### Layout — the alignment toolkit

Twenty layout tools that solve the "manually nudging shapes for fifteen minutes" problem. Once you know them, you stop touching the mouse for layout work entirely.

| # | Tool | What it does |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | Bring to Front | Z-order: shape on top of others |
| 2 | Send to Back | Z-order: shape behind others |
| 3 | Selection Pane | Side panel listing every shape on the slide |
| 4 | Group | Bind selected shapes into a single object |
| 5 | Ungroup | Break a group apart |
| 6 | Regroup | Reapply the last group |
| 7 | Same Width | Make selected shapes equal in width |
| 8 | Same Height | Make selected shapes equal in height |
| 9 | Same Size | Make selected shapes equal in both dimensions |
| 10 | Height | Set explicit height for selected shapes |
| 11 | Width | Set explicit width for selected shapes |
| 12 | Scale | Resize proportionally |
| 13 | Align Left | Anchor selected shapes to the leftmost edge |
| 14 | Align Right | Anchor to the rightmost edge |
| 15 | Align Top | Anchor to the topmost edge |
| 16 | Align Bottom | Anchor to the bottom edge |
| 17 | Align Center | Centre horizontally |
| 18 | Align Middle | Centre vertically |
| 19 | Distribute Horizontally | Equal horizontal spacing across selection |
| 20 | Distribute Vertically | Equal vertical spacing across selection |

<p className="text-xs text-muted-foreground italic mt-[-1.5rem] mb-10">Think-cell layout toolkit. Tools 7–9 (Same Width / Same Height / Same Size) and 13–18 (the alignment family) are the most-used in practice — they are the difference between a slide that looks placed and a slide that looks designed.</p>

And much more beyond these — agendas, libraries, slide workbooks, automated tables of contents, presentation quality checks. The full list is for you to discover; the right way to learn Think-cell is to use it on real slides, not to read the manual front-to-back.

## Kaizen your way — the best-practice checklist

The Japanese principle of **<KeyTerm slug="Kaizen">Kaizen</KeyTerm>** — continuous improvement — is the right mindset for slide-making. Each iteration sharpens the slide a little more. The compounding effect over many revisions is what separates a good slide from a great one.

Nine practices that, applied consistently, hold the bar.

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 1" title="Prioritise visual hierarchy">
    Use font size, bolding, and whitespace to guide the viewer's eye
    in the order you want it to travel the slide.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 2" title="Use templates and shortcuts">
    Leverage PMC templates and keyboard shortcuts to save time and
    ensure consistency.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 3" title="Choose the right chart, simplify visuals">
    Select appropriate chart types for clarity, and avoid clutter by
    focusing only on key data points.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 4" title="Iterate with feedback" emphasize>
    Review, revise, and gather feedback to refine slides to
    perfection. The first draft is never the final draft.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 5" title="Balance content and white space">
    Avoid overcrowding. Leave ample whitespace to make slides
    readable and visually appealing.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 6" title="Keep text concise">
    Use bullet points over full paragraphs, and limit each slide to
    3–5 main points.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 7" title="Use high-quality, relevant images">
    Only use clear, high-resolution images that add value to the
    message. Stock photos that look like stock photos hurt the slide.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 8" title="Align elements carefully">
    Maintain consistent alignment for a clean and balanced look on
    every slide.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Practice 9" title="Craft descriptive titles">
    Use specific titles that summarise each slide's main message,
    rather than generic labels. The title is the conclusion.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

<SelfCheck question="A teammate sends you a slide with a chart titled 'Revenue Growth.' Without seeing the chart, what is wrong with the title?">
The title labels the topic, not the conclusion. "Revenue Growth" tells the reader what the slide is *about* — but a PMC slide title should tell the reader what to *think* about it. Better titles for the same chart would be: "Revenue grew 28% in 2024, well above the 12% sector median," or "Revenue growth has decelerated for three consecutive quarters." The chart is the same; the title is doing the work of communicating the conclusion. Every slide should pass this test before it ships.
</SelfCheck>
