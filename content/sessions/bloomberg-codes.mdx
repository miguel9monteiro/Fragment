## How the terminal actually listens

Everything starts at the search bar, and one mechanic governs it all:

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="The rule" title="Load the security first" emphasize>
    Type the security — <em>BOO LN Equity</em>, say — and load it.
    From that point on, <strong>every function code you run applies to
    the security you have chosen</strong>. DES describes it, FA opens
    its financials, ANR shows its analyst recommendations.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="The alternative" title="Or go function-first">
    You can also type a function code directly, or search by the code
    of a security. The search bar resolves both: functions at the top,
    matching securities below — the same company across its listings,
    depositary receipts, and composites.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

<Callout type="insight" title="Why security-first is the habit to build">
A research session on one name touches a dozen functions — description, financials, holders, credit, relative valuation. Loading the security once and chaining codes is the difference between a fluid twenty-minute workup and re-typing the ticker twelve times. Watch a senior member work a name and this is the rhythm you'll see.
</Callout>

<SelfCheck question="You're building the credit section of a bond pitch — capital structure, maturity wall, ratings. Which codes carry the section?">
CAST for the detailed capital structure, DDIS for the debt distribution — the maturity schedule that tells you whether there's a refinancing wall — and CRPR for the agency ratings, with DRSK/DRAM adding Bloomberg's own default-risk read and its trend. AGGD tells you who the major creditors actually are. That five-code chain is essentially the skeleton of the credit analysis in the club's bond pitches — run on a loaded security, it's a fifteen-minute first pass.
</SelfCheck>

<Callout type="warning" title="On the technical codes">
"We don't do much of it here. Unless you're a Quant and can back-test your pattern…" — the deck's own note, and the club's standing position: technical claims are viewed with skepticism unless they survive a backtest. The backtesting tools are there precisely so that the burden of proof can be met.
</Callout>

## Putting the index to work

A code list becomes a skill when the codes chain into workflows. Three the club runs constantly:

<Pillars>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Workflow 1" title="The new-name workup">
    Load the security → DES for the business → PIB and BICO for the
    company and industry read → FA for the financials → OWN and ANR
    for who holds it and what the street thinks → EQRV to see it
    against peers. Twenty minutes to a defensible first impression.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Workflow 2" title="The macro morning">
    ECO for what prints today → ECSU for how the data has been
    surprising → WIRP for what the market already prices → FOMC after
    a meeting. The macro section of any pitch starts from these four.
  </Pillar>
  <Pillar eyebrow="Workflow 3" title="The credit pass">
    SRCH to find the bonds → YAS to compute the yield → CAST, DDIS,
    and CRPR for structure, maturities, and ratings → DRSK for the
    default-risk overlay. The chain behind every fixed-income pitch
    in the book.
  </Pillar>
</Pillars>

<SelfCheck question="A teammate wants to know whether the market has already priced in the next central-bank cut before writing their rates paragraph. Which code do they run first?">
WIRP — the interest rates incorporated in the market valuation. It shows what the market currently implies for policy rates, which is the baseline any rates view must be stated against: a pitch that predicts a cut the market already fully prices is predicting nothing. ECO tells you when the decision lands and FOMC what was decided last time, but WIRP is the "what's priced in" answer.
</SelfCheck>
