## What the team is arguing

The opening slide commits to a clear, defensible thesis in four lines. Each
of the four is a falsifiable claim — that's the test of a good thesis
slide.

The thesis is not "ITRI is cheap." It is **why** ITRI is cheap — because
the market is pricing a software business as hardware. That's a claim that
can be wrong. A claim that can be wrong is a claim worth pitching.

<Callout type="insight" title="What makes a good thesis slide">
A good thesis slide passes three tests. (1) Each line is a falsifiable
claim, not a description. (2) The claims are independent — they don't all
depend on the same underlying assumption. (3) A reasonable bear could
disagree with you, line by line, and you'd still have a defensible reply.
</Callout>

## The number under the microscope

When you arrive at the valuation slide, the question every senior will ask
is the same: **how much of the upside depends on the multiple, and how
much depends on the cash flows themselves?**

The right way to read this slide: the **base case is mid-table**. The bull
and bear scenarios are not extremes; they are both inside the realistic
WACC and growth bands. That is exactly how a sensitivity should look. If
your base case is at the bull edge, you are anchoring a thesis on
optimism, not analysis.

## What seniors will ask

When the team walks into Q&A, expect these in roughly this order:

1. **"What does the market know that you don't?"** — the counterfactual
   question. The team's answer should be: the market is mis-categorising
   the company. The hardware/software mix has shifted faster than the
   coverage has updated.
2. **"What's the implied software multiple at your fair value?"** — testing
   the SOTP. Be ready with a number like "implied 14x EBITDA on the
   software segment, vs. 18x SaaS peer median."
3. **"What breaks the thesis?"** — testing the bear case. Honest answer:
   if AMI 2.0 timelines slip by 18+ months, the catalyst evaporates and
   the multiple stays compressed.
4. **"Why now?"** — testing the catalyst calendar. The team should be able
   to point to the next four quarters of utility RFP awards as the
   re-rating drivers.

## What worked, what to do differently

What worked: the thesis slide commits to a clear, falsifiable view, and
the valuation sensitivity is honest. The deck does not hide its assumptions.

What to do differently: the team's bear case slide (not shown) is too
soft. The strongest bear thesis is "the software margin is structurally
overstated by allocations from the hardware segment." That argument
deserves a full slide and a numerical rebuttal — not a bullet on a risks
page.

The lesson, for the next pitch: **steelman the bear case explicitly**. A
deck that handles its strongest opposition is more persuasive than a deck
that handles ten weak ones.
